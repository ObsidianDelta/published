Conversation 4 — the plan

What Jordan brings

A draft hinge question for a Grade 3 lesson on two-digit addition with regrouping: 24 + 18. Before you meet, he writes the predicted wrong answers first, explains the misconception story behind each one, and sketches a three-level fading plan.

The two distractors worth keeping

Work through each one as a diagnostic signal, not a grading category.

  • 312 — column addition without regrouping: student adds 4 + 8 = 12, writes “12”; adds 2 + 1 = 3, writes “3”; reads left-to-right as 312. This reveals a place-value gap: the student is not treating digits as positional holders.
  • 32 — partial regrouping: student adds 4 + 8 = 12, writes the 2 but drops the carried ten, then adds 2 + 1 = 3. The procedure exists; the regrouped ten is not conserved.

Ask Jordan: what would you say to the student who wrote 312 that you would not say to the student who wrote 32? That question makes the diagnostic function concrete.

The fading plan (three levels)
  • Level 1 — model it together: work through a parallel problem at the board; think aloud every place-value decision.
  • Level 2 — prompt card: student works from a card showing the steps. Jordan watches and does not speak.
  • Level 3 — one phrase: “check your tens” — then step back.

Contingent support (Wood, Bruner and Ross, 1976) is read from the student, not imposed by the plan. The fading levels are a starting position. Jordan needs to know he can move up a level if the student stalls — that is not failing, that is reading the room.

What to say — and what to hold back
  • Say: “Write the wrong answers before you write the question. Each one is a student’s thinking made visible.”
  • Say: “Your fading plan starts at level 1, but where you actually start depends on what you saw last lesson.”
  • Hold back: do not correct his question’s phrasing until he has explained why he chose it. Hear the reasoning first.
Your turn to lead

Jordan knows his students’ world in ways no pre-service textbook reaches. In conversation 3 he showed you that a trap line of 24 lobster traps plus 18 more lands as clearly as any classroom addition problem — and more truthfully for his students. Ask him to walk you through two or three of those contexts. Let him run that segment of the conversation. Tell him plainly that you are taking his examples into your pre-service seminar. That is not a compliment; it is a fact. He teaches you here.

Where Jordan sits in his own ZPD — as a teacher

In three conversations Jordan has moved through the ZPD for formative assessment at a pace most first-year teachers do not manage. He can construct a hinge question, anticipate misconceptions, and now articulate the fading principle. What remains in his ZPD: reading a mixed-misconception whiteboard set in real time and adjusting mid-lesson without freezing. That is the MKO function for conversation 5 — scaffold real-time responsiveness, then plan your own exit from the mentoring relationship.

Observation lens — for your next classroom visit

Watch for: does Jordan differentiate his response to 312 versus 32, or does he treat all wrong answers the same way? Does he return to level 1 when a student stalls, or stay at the planned level? Does he read when to pull a small group versus re-teach the class? These are the in-the-moment formative assessment moves Black and Wiliam (1998, “Inside the Black Box”) describe as requiring responsive, not scripted, teaching.

Note: Jordan already reads his community with precision. Do not scaffold what he already does.

When it’s tricky

If Jordan arrives with more than three distractors and wants to use all of them:
Ask him to explain the misconception story behind each one, step by step. If he cannot trace the error pathway, the distractor is not useful yet. Keep only the ones he can explain. (The worked example for this scenario discards any answer he cannot account for — not because it might not occur, but because a distractor without an explanation is just a wrong answer.)
If he wants to design the hinge question for a different topic:
Fine — the framework holds for any domain. Ask him to write the wrong answers first. If he can predict two with distinct misconception stories, the question is a genuine hinge question. If he cannot predict any, it is a test item, not a diagnostic tool.
If Jordan is anxious that fading feels like abandoning a struggling student:
Name what Vygotsky’s ZPD actually describes: the space between what a learner does alone and what they do with support. The goal of the MKO is to make themselves unnecessary within that space. Fading is purposeful care, not withdrawal.
If Jordan treats the fading levels as a fixed sequence:
Contingent support (Wood, Bruner and Ross) requires reading the moment, not following a script. The fading plan is a starting position. Ask him: what would the student have to show you to make you move back to level 1? That question makes it contingent rather than mechanical.
If you feel stuck on what to ask next:
Jordan knows his class better than you do. Ask: “What did you notice last time you tried this?” His classroom observation is the evidence. Your job is to help him read it.
If the community-contexts segment starts feeling one-directional — like he is presenting for you:
Ask what he is still figuring out about those contexts, not just what you want to learn. This is peer knowledge exchange, not an interview.

A worked example to build from

24 + 18 = ?

The answer is 42. Two answers show up often on whiteboards:

  • 312 — student adds 4 + 8 = 12, writes “12”; adds 2 + 1 = 3, writes “3”; reads them as 312. They haven’t figured out that the digit in the tens column means ten times as much.
  • 32 — student adds 4 + 8 = 12, writes the 2 but loses the extra ten, then adds 2 + 1 = 3. Almost right — just one ten went missing.

Different answer, different problem, different next step.

A three-level fading plan (example)
  • Level 1 — model it together: work through a similar question at the board, thinking out loud every step.
  • Level 2 — prompt card: student works from a card with the steps on it. You watch; you don’t speak.
  • Level 3 — one phrase only: say “check your tens” and step back.

You don’t go 1, 2, 3 in order. You start where the student is today. You watch for when to move. This has a name: fading. The help gets smaller on purpose, because the goal is that they get there — not that you got them there.

Questions you might have

What if most of my class gets it wrong — do I re-teach the whole lesson?

If more than half the class wrote a wrong answer, re-teaching the whole group makes sense. But be specific about what you’re re-teaching — not “the lesson” but “the part where you carry the ten.” A vague re-teach doesn’t fix the specific gap.

What if they got it wrong in two different ways?

Then you have two groups with two different problems. Re-teaching the whole class will reach one group and talk past the other. It’s worth splitting them — even briefly — so each group hears what they actually need.

How do I know when a student is ready for less help?

Watch for: they stop asking what to do next. They slow down before a tricky step — that’s thinking, not waiting. They start checking their own work. Any of those is a signal to step back.

What if I step back and they get stuck again?

Normal, and fine. Move back to the level that worked — maybe the prompt card again, maybe one phrase. You’re not starting over. You’re just at where they are right now, and you’ll try stepping back again later.

Why plan the wrong answers before asking the question?

When you’re at the front of the class with twenty-two whiteboards in the air, you have about two seconds to decide what to do. If you’ve already thought it through, you know what you’re looking for. If you haven’t, you freeze — exactly what happened in conversation 2.

Do I have to plan for every possible wrong answer?

No — just the likely ones. Two or three is enough. If a student writes something unexpected, ask them to show you their thinking. The plan is for the predictable mistakes that show up again and again.

Why does the help have to get smaller — can’t I just help as much as they need?

You can, and sometimes that’s exactly right. But if the amount of help never changes, students practise doing it with you rather than on their own. The goal is that they do it alone — so they need practice with a little less each time.

Isn’t that just letting them struggle?

There’s a difference between a struggle that moves and one that goes nowhere. Fading means staying close, watching, and stepping back in if they hit a real wall. You’re not walking away. You’re stepping back with your eyes open.
