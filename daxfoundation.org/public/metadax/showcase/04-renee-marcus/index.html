Check-in 4: build the playbook together

What you need before you meet

A shared doc open and ready to edit together. Tell Marcus: "We are going to co-write this; I am just the thinking partner."

How the conversation goes
  • Start with one question: "What would make this team's meetings feel safe to everyone?" Let Marcus answer first.
  • Introduce the "how I like to raise things" round: each team member states their own preference, in their own words. Suggest Marcus frame it as a question to the team, not a form to fill in.
  • Rotating times: ask Marcus to look at where the late slot has landed so far, then ask: "What would fair rotation look like?" He will figure it out.
  • Decision log: pull up check-in 2. Tunde said meetings rarely decided anything. A one-line entry at the end of each meeting doc is all it takes.
  • Pre-meeting ask: connect it to what Joy told you. Marcus can draft a short message he would send the day before to anyone he wants to present something.
What to say

"You noticed the pattern with Joy. Now you are designing the fix with the team. That is the difference between a rule you make for people and a norm they own."

What not to say

Don't say "this is the right way to run meetings." It forecloses the team's voice. Ask questions and let the playbook come from their answers.

Your turn to lead

Joy is going to run the next retrospective using the async retro format she used at her previous company. Marcus is the learner in that meeting. Help him say to the team: "I haven't done it this way before -- Joy's going to take us through it." That sentence, said out loud, changes what the team sees.

Coaching Marcus without taking over

Ask, don't tell. When Marcus gets stuck, try "What do you think is holding this back?" before offering your own view. Amy Edmondson's term for what you are building here is psychological safety: the team's belief that they can raise a concern or take a risk without being penalised. Marcus does not need to use that phrase; he needs to act it into existence.

When it's tricky

If Marcus says

"I already tried asking for input and nobody responded."

Then: Ask when he sent the ask and what form it took. Usually the gap is timing or channel. A one-line post in a busy shared channel gets ignored; a direct note the day before with a specific question gets a reply. Suggest he try both and compare.
If Marcus gets defensive about the Joy incident
Then: Don't debate what happened. Say: "I know you wanted to include her. The good news is she told us exactly what works for her -- most managers never get that." That reframes the moment as useful data.
If the team pushes back on the playbook
Then: That is actually the right sign. If they are pushing back, they are engaged. Help Marcus hear objections as input, not resistance: "What would make this work for you?"
If Marcus wants to write the whole playbook himself
Then: He can draft it -- but he needs to share it as a starting point, not a finished document. Suggest subject line: "Draft for our input round -- please tell me what I got wrong."
If you feel stuck as the guide
Then: That is often a sign you are ahead of Marcus. Come back one step. Ask him where he is, not where you think he should be. Use the "Tell us & ask" tab to get a thinking-partner prompt.
If Joy's retro idea meets resistance from the team
Then: Help Marcus stay the learner. His job in that meeting is to follow Joy's lead and say so. If the team resists the format, that is a retro outcome -- worth naming in the decision log.

Check-in 4: you write it with them

What is happening

You and Renée are building a team playbook together. Not a policy -- a living doc the team owns. Four things go in it.

Item 1 -- how I like to raise things

Send one question to the team: "When you have a concern or an idea, what is the best way to bring it up with me?" Collect their answers. Put them in the doc, one line per person, their words, not yours.

What Joy told you: "Ask me before the meeting if I'm okay to present it, and I'll come ready." That goes in the doc verbatim, with her name on it.

Item 2 -- rotating meeting time

Look at the last two months of standups. Who has held the late slot? Rotate it. No one should always hold the inconvenient time. Document the rotation so the whole team can see it.

Item 3 -- decision log

One line at the end of every meeting doc: Decision: [what was decided]    Owner: [who acts on it]    By: [timeframe]. Tunde said decisions were not landing. This fixes it.

Item 4 -- pre-meeting ask

Before any meeting where you want someone to present: send a direct message the day before. "Hey -- in tomorrow's call I'd like to bring up [topic]. Would you be comfortable presenting your side? No pressure if not." If the answer is no, you present it and name them in the notes.

Joy runs the next retro

What changes when you say it: the team sees that you credit people, that you can be the learner too, and that Joy's expertise matters. Three things land in one sentence.

Joy Leads: async retro

What Joy is doing

Joy ran async retrospectives at her previous company. Everyone writes their notes before the meeting; Joy groups them; the meeting is spent discussing patterns, not reading updates aloud.

Why it works well for a distributed team: people in different time zones contribute their full thinking before the call. The call itself is more substantive.

Your part in that meeting
  • Follow Joy's instructions. She is the facilitator.
  • Ask questions as a participant, not as a manager checking the format.
  • If you want to redirect something, ask Joy first: "Should I weigh in here, or do you want to handle it?"
  • Say at the start: "I haven't done it this way before -- Joy's going to walk us through it."
Questions you might have
  • What if the team keeps directing questions to me during the retro?

    Answer questions about the team's work -- that is still your domain. But if the question is about the retro format, redirect: "That is Joy's call." Maintaining that boundary keeps her authority intact.

    What if they keep doing it even after I redirect?

    Name it directly: "I notice questions keep coming to me -- I want to make sure Joy has the floor here." That one sentence usually resets the dynamic. If it persists, check in with Joy after the meeting: did she feel supported?

  • What if the async format feels slow or awkward?

    Async formats often feel different the first time -- that is normal. Wait until after the retro to form a view. Joy has run this before; your job is to run it once before judging it.

    What if the team says they prefer the old way?

    That is a legitimate outcome. Add it to the decision log and discuss it openly. Joy's expertise is respected whether the team adopts the format or not.

  • What if I feel like I should step in to fix something?

    Pause and ask: is this a safety issue, or is it just uncomfortable? If it is uncomfortable -- silence, disagreement, a slow pace -- that is Joy's retro to manage. Nine times out of ten it is discomfort, not danger.

    How do I tell the difference in the moment?

    Ask yourself: "If I say nothing for 90 more seconds, will someone be harmed?" If no, wait. Joy has run this before. Give her the 90 seconds.
