Your turn to learn — first ten minutes

Liam already knows what box scores mean. You don't, yet. Say it plainly: "Liam, teach me what 5.2 means." He leads this part. You listen, you ask questions, you get things wrong on purpose. This is not a trick — you genuinely don't know and he genuinely does.

Session 4: The Box Score

Today's big idea: a pitcher's whole inning is 3 outs — just his half. That's different from session 1's 6-out inning. If Liam notices that difference himself, stop everything and celebrate it.

What you need

  • Paper and a pencil
  • About 20 minutes
  • Emma if she wants the umpire job

How it goes

1

Liam leads (10 min)

Ask: "What does 5.2 mean?" Let him explain in full. Then: "Could there ever be a 1.3?" When he says the pitcher's whole is 3 outs, not 6, ask: "So the whole changed from last time?" That question is yours to sit with.

Why this works: he cements the concept by explaining it, not by watching you explain it.

2

Write the Jays game

Write four lines: Starter 5.2 · Reliever 1.2 · Reliever 1.1 · Closer 0.1. Ask: "What's the total?"

Why this works: putting it on paper gives him something to point at while he thinks.

3

The decimal trap, together

Add them the wrong way, out loud: 5.2 + 1.2 + 1.1 + 0.1 = 7.6. Write it down. Then: "Does 7.6 innings make sense? How many outs would .6 be?" Let that sit before moving on.

Why this works: finding the wrong answer first makes the right one stick.

4

Count the outs

Count each pitcher's outs: 5×3+2=17, 1×3+2=5, 1×3+1=4, 0×3+1=1. Add them: 17+5+4+1=27. Ask: "How many innings is 27 outs?" He knows. Write 9.

Why this works: outs are his anchor from session 1. This connects new notation to something he already owns.

5

If he's still going (optional)

Try: 6.1 + 1.2 + 0.2 — answer is 8 and 2/3 innings, written 8.2. Then subtraction: starter went 5.2, full game is 9 innings, how much did the bullpen pitch? Answer: 3 and 1/3 innings, written 3.1.

Why this works: same move, different numbers — that's how a skill becomes a habit.

What to say

  • "Liam, teach me what 5.2 means."
  • "Could there ever be a 1.3 in a box score? Why not?"
  • "Does 7.6 innings make sense?"
  • "Count the outs for the starter. What do you get?"

What to skip

  • Don't say "denominator" — just "bottom number".
  • Don't correct his explanation in step 1. Ask questions instead.
  • Don't push past 20 minutes if focus drops.

When it's tricky

If he adds .2 and .2 and writes .4

Don't correct it — ask it: "Can there be 4 outs in an inning?" He'll catch it himself. If not: "Count the outs out loud. What does .2 mean?"

If he gets 7.6 and thinks that's right

Good — that's the trap working. Ask: "Seven-point-six innings. Does that make sense? How many outs is .6?" Don't give the answer. Give him time.

If he goes quiet after a wrong answer

Don't wait for him to explain himself. Move straight to counting outs. Give him something to do with his hands. Emma can call each out as you count.

If you lose track of the outs yourself

Say it out loud: "Let me count these too." Do it together. You working through it beside him is more useful than having it figured out ahead of time.

If Emma wants in

Give her the umpire job: she counts and calls each out aloud as you go. Keep her busy so Liam stays the scorer.

If focus drops after 15 minutes

Stop before the optional problems. The main puzzle — 27 outs = 9 innings — is the win for today. The extra problems can open session 5.

Questions you might have

Why can't the number after the dot be 3 or higher?

Because three outs ends an inning. A pitcher pitching "1.3" innings would mean he got 3 outs — but as soon as he got that third out, the inning was over. So 1.3 = 2.0. There's no .3 in a box score.

What if a pitcher gets exactly 3 outs and starts a new inning?

That counts as one full inning: 1.0. The .0 just means zero extra outs. You'll see "1.0" a lot for relievers who come in, get three outs, and leave.

Why do we count outs instead of just adding the numbers?

Because the numbers after the dot aren't regular decimals — they mean outs (0, 1, or 2). When you add them like regular decimals you get things like .4 or .5 that don't exist. Counting outs and then dividing by 3 always gives the right answer.

Is there a faster way once you get good?

Yes. You can add the whole innings first, then add up just the outs, and convert: every 3 outs = 1 inning. Scorers who do this every day do it in their heads in about two seconds.

How is this different from the six-out inning in session 1?

In session 1 the "whole" was a full inning — both halves, 6 outs. When a pitcher is on the mound, he only pitches one half: 3 outs. So the same word "inning" means something different depending on what you're counting. That's the hardest part, and you already noticed it.

Does that mean fractions can have different wholes?

Exactly. Half a loonie and half a game are both "half," but one is 50 cents and one is 3 outs. The bottom number only makes sense when you know what the whole is. That's true for every fraction, everywhere.

Your turn to lead

You know box scores. Mom doesn't yet. At the start of session 4, you run the first ten minutes. Read this script to her.

You say:

"Mom, look at this. See 5.2? That means the starter pitched 5 full innings and then got 2 more outs. It does NOT mean five-point-two innings like a regular decimal."

She'll ask: "So what's the most the dot number can be?"

You say: "Only 0, 1, or 2. Three outs and you're done. There's no .3."

Then ask her:

"What does 1.2 mean?" Wait. If she says "one-point-two innings," say "nope" and explain it again yourself.

You tell her:

"One inning and 2 outs. Each out is one third of an inning. So 1.2 is one and two-thirds innings."

Then show her the Jays game

Write this out and hand her the pencil:

Starter: 5.2 Reliever: 1.2 Reliever: 1.1 Closer: 0.1

Ask: "What's the total innings?" She'll probably add them as decimals and say 7.6. Tell her: "That's the trap. Count the outs instead." Show her: 17 + 5 + 4 + 1 = 27 outs = 9 innings. Full game.
