What you're looking at

A kit one tutor could make in a sitting

This is a showcase of something a tutor can make for one learner, in one sitting, on their own AI subscription.

It began as an interview. A tutor who taught maths in England, mostly to students of fifteen and sixteen at O-level standard, was asked a few plain questions: what learners find hardest, what he found hardest to teach, and what he wished he had. His answers shaped every part of this kit.

  • The hardest topic: sentences into equations. Learners who can solve 3x + 4 = 19 freeze when the same thing arrives as words.
  • The hardest problem: time. He always felt he was racing the clock.
  • His first idea for fixing it: give the learner more time to explain.

MetaDAX took those answers and built a kit with two sides: one page for the tutor, one for the learner. The learner's world here is football, but that is a setting, not a limit. One line rebuilds it for any interest.

Five sentences

The five sentences

The number machine

“Double last season's goals, then add 5 cup goals, and you get 23.”

2x + 5 = 23

Lesson: undo the last step first.

The balance

“Two scarves and a 4 pound badge cost the same as one scarf and an 11 pound programme.”

2x + 4 = x + 11

Lesson: “costs the same as” is the equals sign.

Shirt numbers

“Three players have shirt numbers in a row. They add up to 72.”

n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) = 72

Lesson: one more each time, not double.

The pitch

“A pitch is 30 m longer than it is wide. Its perimeter is 340 m.”

2w + 2(w + 30) = 340

Lesson: four sides, not two.

The tickets

“Each ticket costs x pounds plus a 3 pound booking fee. Four tickets cost 56 pounds.”

4(x + 3) = 56

Lesson: this is where brackets come from.

About

About this kit

From the tutor's chair: time, handed back

Three routes, chosen before you start.

Quick, Standard or Deep. Each is complete on its own, and each ends with a note that tells the next session where to begin. You never have to finish everything. You only have to stop well.

A fast path.

If the learner gets the first three right first time, skip ahead to the pitch. If they miss two in a row, drop back to the balance. The kit decides nothing for you; it shows you where the doors are.

A translation strip.

The phrases and their signs on one screen: more than, less than, twice, shared equally, costs the same as. The trap that catches everyone is in plain sight: 5 less than x is x - 5, never 5 - x.

Six slips, named in advance.

Every wrong choice in every game is a specific mistake, and your page tells you what to say when it happens: the reversed subtraction, the missing bracket, consecutive numbers written as multiples, the perimeter counted once, half a sentence, the wrong operation.

The pause, built in.

Before every example, the learner says the sentence back in their own words, and you wait. It was the tutor's own idea, and the kit treats it as the method, not a nicety. If you are short of time, cut an example, not the pause.

Continuity.

At the end, tick what happened and add a line in your own words. The page writes a short next-time note. Paste it in next time, and the next session is built from where this one stopped.

From the learner's chair: your world, your pace, your turn

It starts with what you know.

You are the football expert here, and the page says so. Every sentence comes from that world: goals, shirt numbers, ticket prices, the size of a pitch.

One question at a time.

Nothing is marked right before you choose. When you choose, the page tells you what happened, and if it was a slip, which one. “Wrong” becomes “here is the exact thing to fix.”

You move the maths.

Five scenes you can press: a number machine you run backwards, a balance that never tips whatever you take off, bars that share out, and tiles whose brackets open in your hands. The equation changes as you go.

Time to think.

Before every example: say it back first. No timer, no rush.

Then you lead.

At the end you make up a sentence about your own team, and the tutor turns it into algebra while you check them.

The human delta

What only a person can do

The kit can hold the structure: the order, the examples, the slips, the routes. It cannot read a face. It cannot tell the silence of thinking from the silence of being stuck, wait a few seconds longer than is comfortable, praise the sentence instead of the answer, or decide that today should end on something the learner got right.

Those belong to the tutor, and the kit says so on the tutor's page, in a card called What only you can do. MetaDAX calls this the human delta value: what the person brought that the system could not. This kit is built to hand the tutor more of it, not less.
