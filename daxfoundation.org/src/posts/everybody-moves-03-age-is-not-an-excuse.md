Of the four slogans in this series, this is the one most likely to be dismissed as a poster. "Age is not an excuse" sounds like something barked at a person who is tired, and if that were all it was, we would have left it out. We kept it because the material behind it is specific, and because it names the exact group most products give up on — and the exact group our tools are supposed to reach.

The Pump Club's own piece on this does not hedge. It calls "I'm too old for this" "the most dangerous lie in fitness." Then it does the thing a slogan never does: it cites. A twelve-week study in which adults aged 85 and up gained roughly 11% in quadriceps size and about 46% in leg strength — gains comparable to a group thirty years younger. Resistance training associated with preserved muscle, cognition, and brain volume in people over 80. The framing is not vanity; it is independence — standing up from a chair without help, carrying the groceries, getting down on the floor to play with grandchildren. And the close is the line we think about most: it "doesn't care how long you waited. It only cares that you start."

## The oldest person is the real customer

Most movement products are built for the person who is already most of the way there — young, mobile, equipped. The honest test of "for everybody" from the last piece has a sharper version here: who is the *oldest* person your tool actually serves, and does it serve them first or last?

Arnold's own answer is unusually literal, because his movement work points straight at them. The seat Zimmer Biomet created for him in November 2024 is aimed, by the company's own framing, at older adults and joint-replacement patients — the release cited that 65-and-up Americans were about 17% of the population in 2022, projected toward 22% by 2040, and leaned on the standard guidance of strength work twice a week, aerobic work across the week, and balance training specifically for the 65-plus. In Fortune's coverage of that announcement he put the stakes plainly: exercise, he said, "is the only thing that is going to extend your joy for life and your quality of life." And he has said the general version too: "You don't have to be a competitive athlete in order to really make this happen."

He is, as of July 2026, 79, still publishing a workout every day, still shooting. We make no medical claim about anyone and we are not his doctor; the public record simply shows a person in his late seventies treating his own routine as the proof. That is the demographic. Not the exception to "everybody" — the center of it.

## What this asks of a tool

If age is not an excuse, then a tool that only works for the young is the one making the excuse on the user's behalf. So the requirement lands on us, not on the 80-year-old:

- The first movement has to be reachable **from a chair**, with nothing bought — the same floor the Pump Club sets with chair squats and incline pushups.
- The interface cannot assume a quick, trained body. **Re·UI** has to read a slow, careful, seated movement as a real one, not a failed one.
- The **gesture lab** techniques have to be written for the person starting at 70 with two decades off, not only for the person maintaining at 30.
- None of the above can sit behind a price or a form. The person who waited longest has the least patience for a paywall and the most to gain from the first week.

That is a design brief, and it is a hard one. A system that genuinely welcomes the slowest, oldest, least-equipped mover is harder to build than one that quietly filters them out. But that filter is the excuse, dressed up as a product decision, and the whole point of this line is to refuse it.

## Why we give it away, here especially

The economics of "age is not an excuse" are brutal if you are selling. The person who benefits most from starting at 85 is, on average, not the person with the most disposable income for a fitness subscription, and a business has to notice that. A gift does not have to. That is the cleanest argument for our posture, and it is strongest exactly here: hand the tools over, free, cognition provided by Obsidian Delta, and the question of whether a given older adult is a profitable customer never gets to override the question of whether movement would help them. It would. So we remove the toll.

The line, then, is not a bark at a tired person. It is a promise to build for the person the market skips, and to give it to them, because — his words, and we believe them — the thing it buys back is joy for life and quality of life, and it does not care how long you waited. It only cares that you start.
