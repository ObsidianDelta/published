When a company that built telephone systems for taxi fleets starts talking about donating its intellectual property to a foundation, the natural reading is that it found a warmer subject and pivoted into it. That is not what happened here. The clearest way to show it is to go back twenty-two years and lay out, in order, the same idea being attempted four times.

The idea is simple to state and has been hard to build every single time: get companies to put their reach behind a cause, in a way that everyone can see, with someone in the middle holding each party to what it agreed to. That is all. It is not an AI idea. It predates everything technical on this site by more than a decade.

## What the founder saw

Two things started it, and they belong to one person. Obsidian Delta's founder, Jason Jeyanandan, saw them, and has asked that they be set down plainly.

In 2004, after the Indian Ocean tsunami, he saw a photograph of a man in the aftermath. What he took from it was a practical problem: a person who wants to help has no good way to tell which charities are actually doing the work. So he built **AllCharities.org** — a site that gathered third-party reporting on what charities were doing on the ground, so that a decision to give could rest on evidence rather than on advertising. This was 2004. GiveWell did not exist yet. AllCharities.org had the evidence and no reach.

The second thing was Somalia. During the 2011 famine he saw two parents on television. They had walked a week through the drought to reach a hospital. Their baby had died. It was in a bundle beside them. He kept a picture of Somalia on his Facebook for a long time after.

He names that as one of the forces behind **1Click1Life** — a platform meant to solve the reach problem that AllCharities.org never did. People were not failing to care; they were tired of the news and had nowhere to put the caring. So the mechanism was a game: follow it, and each post carried a clue to real buried treasure put up by businesses, and alongside the clue, something true about what was happening and a way to act. Learning was the price of the clue. No money ever passed through it; the businesses' contributions went up in public.

Both are over now. He calls them an epic failure that never got rolling, and we are not going to soften that, because the failure is the point of this piece.

## The part that was never written down

Underneath 1Click1Life was a principle with a name: **corporate social synergy**. Separate parties — companies, charities, a community, and a neutral party in the middle — each getting more together than any of them could alone, each with a declared responsibility, and the party in the middle holding the whole thing to its word.

Here is the part that has not been said in public until now, and it is the hinge of the whole story. The "corporations" in corporate social synergy were not an abstraction. They were his own taxi clients.

This was not theory running under a treasure-hunt app. It was something he actually did. The taxi business is an infrastructure business: a fleet is already woven into a city, through its cars, its drivers, and its phone lines. Our client **Blue and White** in Mississauga ran around three hundred vehicles across that one city. So he did community outreach for clients like that — finding ways for a business already serving a community to draw support back from it by putting its reach behind a cause. A fleet that already answered three thousand calls a day could carry something into the same city those calls came from. The company that wired the phones was the same company trying to wire the giving.

So the count is four, not two. AllCharities.org in 2004. 1Click1Life after Somalia. Corporate social synergy, practised directly with the taxi fleets. And now a fourth attempt, which we will come to. One idea. Four runs at it. Twenty-two years.

## The original documents are lost

We should be honest about the state of the record. The original write-ups of corporate social synergy are gone. What survives is a set of short videos recorded in 2021, on two YouTube channels — one for 1Click1Life, one for AllCharities.org — explaining the principle, the benefit to each party, and the responsibility of each party. They are on the founder's own account. The clearest single one is still up: [What is Corporate Social Synergy?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cpF4J21X_5Q) The full set across the [1Click1Life channel](https://www.youtube.com/@1click1life32/videos) is public; the companion set on the AllCharities.org channel exists and is being recovered. Where a claim in this post rests only on a lost document, treat the videos as the receipt.

## Why it failed — which was not the idea

It is tempting, looking back at a thing that never got rolling, to conclude the idea was wrong. We do not think it was, and the reason matters.

A synergy is a promise that everyone wins once everyone shows up. Until everyone shows up, it is a pitch. And there was no way to make the showing-up real. A business could say it would put part of its marketing behind a cause every month. There was no durable, shared record of that commitment that the community and the charity could both see and check. There was no neutral party that could actually hold each side to what it signed, beyond one man asking nicely. The commitments lived in documents that could be quietly changed or quietly dropped — the same documents that are now, fittingly, lost.

The idea needed infrastructure to run on, and the infrastructure did not exist. Not the goodwill, not the market, not the moral case — the plumbing. A way for separate parties to declare in advance what they will and will not do, have those declarations recorded where they cannot be edited afterward, and let anyone who cares check conduct against them.

That is the thing that was missing in 2011. It is also a precise description of what we spent the intervening decade building.

## The decade of building infrastructure

None of what came next looks like charity work. In 2015 our first product, Delta Suite, went live for the taxi industry — enterprise telephony, dispatch, call automation, its voice work forged in coordination with Microsoft Research. Obsidian Delta was incorporated in 2017. For years the work was voice automation for fleets, and then a platform rebuilt more than once.

But read the shape of it rather than the subject. The whole decade was spent learning how to make separate parties — a caller, a dispatcher, a fleet, a system acting on someone's behalf — coordinate reliably and leave a record of what each was bound to. That is the taxi problem and it is also, exactly, the corporate-social-synergy problem. The subject changed from giving to dispatch and the structure did not move an inch.

It ends in a protocol. The [Constraint Protocol](https://github.com/daxfoundation/constraint-protocol) is a formal way for two parties to declare constraints in advance, sign what they do, write it to an append-only record, and keep the party that judges separate from the party that acts. Published in full and openly licensed, at [daxfoundation.org/#cp](https://daxfoundation.org/#cp). It is a working draft with one operational run behind it, and we say so.

Set it beside 2011 and it is the missing plumbing, line for line. Declared responsibilities. A record that cannot be quietly edited. A party in the middle that holds the integrity instead of hoping for it. Corporate social synergy did not fail because nobody wins when everyone shows up. It failed because there was no way to make everyone's showing-up visible and binding. Now there is.

## The fourth attempt

Which brings us to the thing that looks like a pivot and is not.

Obsidian Delta is in the process of donating intellectual property to the [DAX Foundation](https://daxfoundation.org). That sentence is the fourth run at the 2004 idea, and it is the most literal one yet.

The logic is the one from the 2016 outreach, turned on ourselves. In corporate social synergy, a company commits to a cause, and a neutral party in the middle holds that commitment — because the party that makes a promise should not also be the only party that gets to decide whether it was kept. That was 1Click1Life's job in the plan. It is now structural. A company can commit to a constraint; it should not also be the body that holds the constraint. So the work that holds it sits in the Foundation, apart from the company. The neutral party in the middle, which in 2011 was one overcommitted man picking up litter in a snowstorm, is finally a thing that exists outside him.

This is the whole argument, and it is unmistakable once the four are in a row. 2004, evidence for giving. 2011, reach for giving. The taxi years, the practice of it with real clients. And now, a company placing what it built where the company itself cannot quietly take it back. The domain kept changing. The idea never did.

## What is not here

In the register we hold ourselves to on this blog, here is what this post does not contain and does not claim.

It does not claim any of the first three attempts succeeded. They did not. AllCharities.org and 1Click1Life never got rolling, and the corporate-social-synergy outreach did not scale past the fleets it was tried on.

It does not offer numbers. We have no verified user counts, donation totals, or participating-charity figures for AllCharities.org or 1Click1Life. The original documentation of corporate social synergy is lost; the 2021 videos are the surviving record, and part of that record is still being recovered.

It does not tell you the DAX Foundation is an incorporated entity, a registered charity, or anything that could issue a receipt. It is not. Obsidian Delta is *in the process of* donating intellectual property to it. That process is under way, not finished, and we will say when it is.

And it does not claim the fourth attempt has worked. The Constraint Protocol has run in full once. The donation is in progress. This is an idea with twenty-two years and three failures behind it, being attempted a fourth time with, at last, something underneath it to run on. Whether that is enough is not yet a thing we are entitled to say. When it has run, we will say how many times.
