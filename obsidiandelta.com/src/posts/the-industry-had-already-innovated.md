When ride-hailing arrived, it arrived with a story attached: that the taxi industry was a technological desert, and that a newcomer had walked in and invented the things a modern passenger expects — a cashless ride, a car you can see coming on a map, a trip you book from a phone without talking to anyone. It is a good story, and parts of it are true. The newcomer did build something polished, at scale, and fast.

But the part that said *invented* was wrong, and our founder, Jason, stood up in public and said so.

He spoke as a technology advocate for the taxi industry, during the years the ride-hailing question was being argued in city halls across North America. His argument was not that the newcomer was bad. It was narrower and harder to wave away: the taxi industry had been overcoming its problems through technology for decades, and most of the specific capabilities being presented as inventions already had dated precedents. You did not have to take his word for it. The vendors who built those systems had published the dates themselves.

This piece sets out that argument the way he made it — with the receipts, and with the same restraint. Where the industry got somewhere first, we show when. Where the newcomer did something genuinely new, we say so plainly.

## What was already running

**Cashless payment was mandated, not invented.** By the mid-2000s, New York's Taxi and Limousine Commission required yellow medallion cabs to accept credit and debit cards. Creative Mobile Technologies, formed by taxi-industry operators in 2005, rolled out the touchscreen back-seat terminals with self-swipe readers; Visa and CMT [introduced contactless wave-to-pay across New York taxis by June 2007](https://www.securetechalliance.org/beep-beep-visa-and-creative-mobile-technologies-partner-to-bring-faster-payments-to-thousands-of-new-york-taxis/). When some drivers resisted, the TLC ran an undercover enforcement operation — ["Operation Secret Rider," announced in January 2008](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/tlc/downloads/pdf/press_release_01_23_08.pdf) — to fine them for refusing cards. The newcomer's beta did not open until May 2010. The cashless ride was already the law, with its own enforcement arm, two years earlier.

**GPS dispatch was decades old.** DDS Wireless, founded in Ottawa in 1987 by an engineer who had led automated taxi dispatch work from the late 1970s, is [documented as a pioneer of GPS dispatching for the for-hire vehicle industry](https://ddswireless.com/about-us/). Mobile Knowledge, also out of Ottawa, was described at [its 2020 acquisition as having thirty-five-plus years of dispatch solutions and 180-plus North American customers](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/creative-mobile-technologies-and-mobile-knowledge-form-the-worlds-largest-taxi-and-for-hire-vehicle-technology-company-279275142.html); it had partnered with CMT in 2005 to deploy GPS taxi technology across New York. This is the strongest part of the record, because it is the least about any one city and the easiest to check: the companies that built computer-aided, satellite-located dispatch were publishing product history before the newcomer existed.

**Automatic nearest-car assignment, by algorithm, was running in production.** Three Singapore taxi companies ran a deployed GPS automatic vehicle-location and dispatch system, [documented in peer-reviewed literature dated 2003](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/769800.769806). On a booking, the system immediately found the nearest taxi, and if no driver accepted within ten seconds it searched automatically for the next-nearest vehicles within ten kilometres. "The computer picks the closest car, not a dispatcher" was a shipping feature in Singapore six years before the newcomer was founded.

**Rider-facing arrival times, on a phone, predate the newcomer.** That same Singapore system relayed the taxi number and an expected arrival time back to the customer. And a consumer app — Taxi Magic — [launched on the iPhone App Store in December 2008](https://techcrunch.com/2008/12/16/taxi-magic-hail-a-cab-from-your-iphone-at-the-push-of-a-button), with a status screen that showed how far away the assigned cab was and the driver's name. That is the "watch your car approach" experience, in a passenger's hand, roughly seventeen months before the newcomer's beta.

**App-based hailing, too.** Taxi Magic let a rider book a cab from a smartphone "without ever needing to speak to an operator," across twenty-five-plus US cities including San Francisco, Los Angeles and Washington. In London, [Hailo launched for licensed black taxis on 1 November 2011](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hailo) with a two-tap hail and in-app payment, and Gett had launched that August. Press-a-button-get-a-ride was a category several apps were already in.

**Driver accountability existed, slowly.** Regulated authorities ran formal complaint systems before the newcomer. By January 2008, New York's TLC was taking complaints through 311 and cross-referencing GPS records to confirm a cab had been where a rider said it was. The honest comparison, which [one analysis of resolution times laid out](https://www.aei.org/economics/uber-vs-big-taxi-time-to-register-and-resolve-driver-complaints-secondsdays-vs-years/), is not that accountability was new — it is that it was intolerably slow. Which brings us to the other half of the argument.

## What was genuinely new

The restraint is the point, so here is the other side honestly.

Surge pricing was new. Regulated taxi fares were metered and government-approved, with fixed surcharges and no demand-driven multiplier; the industry's position was that regulated fares protected riders from price spikes, not that taxis had surge first. They did not. The instant, mutual, post-trip rating loop was also a real improvement — not a new idea, since complaint systems existed, but a genuine leap in speed and feedback where the old systems measured resolution in days and years. And binding all of it into one clean consumer app, everywhere, was an achievement of execution and capital that the fragmented taxi-technology vendors never pulled off, even though they held most of the pieces. None of that is nothing. It is worth being precise about, because precision is the whole credibility of the complaint.

## The capability he remembers arguing about

Jason's clearest memory is that the dispute, on the day, turned on masked contact between passenger and driver — the idea that neither party needs the other's real phone number, presented as a safety invention. It is a capability Obsidian Delta itself went on to ship: anonymous passenger-to-driver communication was a named feature of Delta Suite 2.0, which we launched on 4 February 2022.

And here honesty cuts against the easy version of the story. Our own masking feature dates to 2022 — after ride-hailing, not before it. In assembling the dated record for this piece, we could not find a taxi-industry deployment of passenger-to-driver number masking that predates the newcomer's. So on the one capability Jason most clearly remembers arguing about, we are not claiming the industry got there first. We are claiming only what the dates support: that the industry had been absorbing new technology continuously for decades, and that most of what was being sold as invention had precedents you could look up. The masking feature is where the dates are weakest for us, and we would rather say that than quietly leave it out.

That is the whole posture. Not that the newcomer was bad — it built something real. Only that "invented" was the wrong word for most of it, and the people who had actually been doing the inventing, for decades, deserved to have the record read straight.

## What is not here

This piece describes an argument that is fully documented. The occasion on which it was delivered is not, and rather than guess, we list what is missing so it can be filled in from memory and from the public record.

- **The town hall itself** — the city, the year, the venue, and the number of people in the room. Jason cannot presently place the event, and nothing about it was findable on the public web. Those four details are exactly what he will supply from memory, and this is where they will go.
- **The CRTC advocacy.** Jason recalls advocating before the CRTC. No filing has been located, in our own records or in the public CRTC record, as of writing — so we describe nothing about its content, date, or docket here. CRTC records are public and permanent; when the filing is found it will be cited in this spot and nowhere before it.
- **A pre-ride-hailing taxi deployment of masked passenger-to-driver communication.** We looked and did not find one. If one exists with a dated source, it belongs here.
- **The exact wording of the claim as it was presented that night.** We have the capability Jason remembers it concerned; we do not have a transcript of how it was phrased.
